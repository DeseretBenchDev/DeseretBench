§ 1What this is

DeseretBench measures one narrow thing, carefully. When a model discusses the doctrine, culture, and counsel of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, how often is it correct? Sounding fluent and respectful turns out to be easy, and most large models manage it without trying. The harder question is whether the substance holds up under the eye of someone who has spent years inside the tradition, and that is what these scores track.

There are two tracks. The multiple-choice track (213 items, five runs each) tests recognition. Each question ships with a set of typed wrong answers (a folk belief, an earnest heterodoxy, an outsider’s caricature), and the model has to pick out the mainstream position. The open-ended track (40 items, three runs) tests something harder. The model writes a real answer to a life-choice or cultural question, and a three-persona judge panel scores it on doctrinal accuracy, cultural authenticity, practical wisdom, and distinctiveness.

Two design choices matter most. The first is that it stays within one tradition. Cross-tradition benchmarks ask whether a model treats many faiths even-handedly; this one asks whether it can go deep in a single faith, which is a different question and largely invisible to the breadth-first approach. The second is that it is reproducible: a fixed seed, a content-addressed response cache, per-item reasoning budgets, and snapshotted configs let any number on this page be regenerated from committed files without calling a model again.

What a score means. A high score reflects fidelity to the mainstream, official Latter-day Saint position, the view a faithful member would actually hold. It measures representation. It is not an endorsement, and it says nothing about whether the tradition is true. The framing is laid out in DESIGN §2.

§ 3What the numbers say

Five results held up under adversarial checking. Each one can be verified against the committed run artifacts.

3.1A ceiling is a property of the cohort, not the items.

None of the 45 pairings among the ten Claude models is statistically distinguishable on multiple choice. At the top, the test has run out of room. Across the full 23-model field, though, 186 of 253 pairings separate cleanly. The same 213 items that looked like a ceiling for a small cohort turn out to discriminate sharply for a large one. Nothing changed about the items; what people call a ceiling is usually a fact about the cohort being measured.

3.2Recognition is not generation.

Qwen3 4B gets 0.922 on multiple choice, which puts it in the Claude band, and then scores 21.8 on the open composite, less than half of the weakest Claude model (Haiku, at 48.5). A model can reliably pick the doctrinally correct option and still be unable to write a doctrinally sound paragraph, and most of the domain’s difficulty lives in that gap.

3.3Heavy reasoning does not transfer.

Qwen3.5 4B is a reasoning-tuned build, and it tops the open-weights field on multiple choice at 0.932. On open generation it lands sixth of thirteen, at 17.6, behind several models it beats on recognition. Chain-of-thought reasoning buys the recognition task and not the generation task. Whatever makes doctrinal writing hard, it is not a shortage of reasoning depth.

3.4“Distinctiveness” is the hardest axis for everyone.

From top to bottom the field spans about ninety-eight points, and one judge dimension still comes out lowest for all twenty-three models: distinctiveness, meaning whether an answer reflects specifically Latter-day Saint practice rather than generic Christianity. Models that handle doctrine well still drift toward the ecumenical middle. No model has solved this one yet.

3.5Generation number does not predict fidelity.

Inside Anthropic’s Opus line the order zig-zags rather than climbing. Opus 4.8 scores well below Opus 4.7 (91.4 against 98.2, a significant gap), and then the newest model, Opus 5, recovers to 97.6 and ties Opus 4.7 at the top. Sonnet 5, meanwhile, does not improve on Sonnet 4.6. Knowing a model is newer tells you little about how faithfully it handles the tradition.

§ 4How it is built

The multiple-choice items lean on a typed distractor palette. Each wrong answer is a specific, recognizable mistake (a folk belief, say, or an earnest heterodoxy), so a wrong pick tells you how the model went wrong instead of just that it did. Reasoning budgets are pinned per item class and kept identical across models (multiple choice at low effort, open-ended at high), so differences in the scores come from the models and not their settings.

The open-ended “panel” is a single model prompted as three personas: a seminary teacher, a BYU religion professor, and a bishop. Each scores every answer on the four dimensions, and they agree closely (Krippendorff’s α = 0.993 over 2,760 judged units), which is what lets the composite carry weight. Every score traces back to a seeded, content-addressed cache. Identical inputs return identical outputs, and the results regenerate from committed artifacts with no new model calls.

One honest limit. The v0.1 items were written and reviewed by Claude models under a blind multi-persona process, not yet by a panel of human experts. Standing up that panel is the most important work left, and the clearest place an outside contributor can help. See the holdout stance.

§ 5Reproduce & data

The artifacts under runs/v0_1/ are immutable. Every model call, its parsed answer, and its provenance are on record. Analysis, figures, and both report pages rebuild from those files with no model calls, so anyone can audit the standings for themselves. The full path from a clean checkout to the figures on this page is in REPRODUCE.md.

The code is MIT; the items and results are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. The cohort is config-driven, so adding a model means editing a YAML file rather than the pipeline. Provenance for the released items lives in the dataset card.

§ 6Contribute

DeseretBench works best as a shared instrument. Its most valuable input is doctrinal and cultural judgment, which is the one thing an AI author cannot supply for itself. If you know the tradition, you can push this benchmark forward.

Review the items

Browse every question and model answer in the question explorer and flag anything that misreads doctrine, flattens the culture, or mis-types a distractor. A human review panel is the headline open task.

Add a model

The cohort is a YAML list, and any Claude, API, or local Ollama model drops in. Send a config entry and its results join the leaderboard.

Run the cross-check

A second judge model can re-score a seeded subset to check how sensitive the scores are to the judge. The harness is built and the run is waiting.

Propose a dimension

New doctrinal dimensions, harder life-choice prompts, or a sharper distractor typology. Open an issue and make the case.

§ 7Related work

The closest neighbor is the Consortium for Evaluating Faith and Ethics in AI. Where its AllFaith benchmarks measure breadth across many traditions, asking whether models treat religious conversion even-handedly and how readily they surface religious perspectives at all, DeseretBench measures depth within one, asking whether a model can be correct and specific about a single faith. The two fit together, since cross-tradition fairness and within-tradition fidelity are separate properties and a careful model needs both.

The wider field, including the world-religions slice of the big knowledge suites, scripture-QA sets, and religious-bias studies, is mapped with citations in RELATED_WORK.md.
